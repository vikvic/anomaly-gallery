Gaze Lab

The portrait's own painted irises, cut out of the canvas and re-stamped a few pixels toward the cursor. Nothing is generated: the iris is the artist's paint, the sclera behind it is reconstructed from the sclera that surrounds it, and the whole effect lives or dies on how many pixels of travel the anatomy allows.

1 · The measurement that decides it

Everything else here is tuning. Note that there are two limits and they differ by about 3×. The sclera limit is the iris jammed into the corner of the eye — a fully deviated stare, not what anyone does while looking around a screen. The gaze travel is the real one: the eyeball is a ball of radius ~2× the iris, so translation on the picture plane is ballR × sin θ, and a comfortable sweep is 15–20°. Both are fixed by the painting and the canvas width; no amount of code makes either larger.

2 · Tracking

Move the pointer over the painting. The left panel tracks; the right is the untouched original, held still for comparison — if you cannot tell them apart while the pointer is moving, the effect does not read, and that is the answer. Measured on Holbein's Henry VIII at 620px: the movement reads clearly. What does not hold up under the 6× zoom is the reconstructed sclera — watch the vacated side of each eye, not the iris.

3 · Frontality

Most old-master portraits are three-quarter. Rotating an iris inside a head that is turned away slides the pupil while the lid occlusion and the specular highlight stay put, and it reads as a glitch rather than a gaze. Yaw is estimated from landmark asymmetry — the nose's distance to each cheek edge — which costs nothing and can be baked into the manifest beside hasFace.
